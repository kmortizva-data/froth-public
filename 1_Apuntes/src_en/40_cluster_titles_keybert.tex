\cabecera{40}{Cluster titles that mean something: KeyBERT (not trimmed sentences)}

\section{The problem (you spotted it)}

Version 1 of the subtitles took the cluster's most central \emph{sentence} and cut it at 12
words. Result: headings like ``Considering both\ldots'', ``In the present st\ldots'',
fragments that hint at the topic but feel chopped off, like a half-read page. And the old
keywords (c-TF-IDF: ``dpa, dda, lepidolite'') were no better: they classify well but they
\emph{do not communicate}.

\section{How the field solves it: KeyBERT}

The standard (KeyBERT, and BERTopic's representation layer) does not trim sentences: it
extracts short \textbf{keyphrases} and ranks them by MEANING.

\begin{concepto}{KeyBERTInspired in 3 steps}
\textbf{1. Candidates}: 2 to 3 word n-grams from the cluster's text (with the same hardened
vectoriser from \texttt{cluster.py}: no digits, chemical elements spared). \\
\textbf{2. Rank}: embed each candidate with SPECTER and measure its cosine to the cluster's
\emph{centroid}. \\
\textbf{3. Diversify}: MMR so the chosen phrases are not nearly identical.
\end{concepto}

The elegant part: these are the SAME pieces Froth already had (SPECTER $+$ cosine $+$ centroid
$+$ MMR from Phase 7). That is why it was built by hand, with no new dependency, the project's
pattern: build each piece in order to understand it.

\section{Two stumbles that teach (your real data)}

The first attempt produced titles with OCR noise (``limu'', ``nmic'', ``cgfs'') and generic ones
(``ore flotation'' where it should have said ``lepidolite flotation''). Two fixes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\texttt{min\_df} $\geq$ 2}: require the phrase to appear in SEVERAL papers of
        the cluster. OCR rubbish lives in a single abstract $\to$ out.
  \item \textbf{Contrastive score} (the c-TF-IDF idea, but in embedding space):
        \[
        \mathrm{score} = \cos(\mathrm{phrase}, \mathrm{centroid}_c)
                       - 0.4 \, \cos(\mathrm{phrase}, \mathrm{centroid}_{\mathrm{global}})
        \]
        It rewards what is close to THIS cluster and far from the whole corpus. That is how
        ``lepidolite flotation'' (specific) beats ``ore flotation'' (generic, close to the
        centre of everything).
\end{itemize}

\begin{ojo}
The keyphrase closest to the centroid is NOT the best title: it tends to be the most generic.
Specificity (far from the global average) is what makes a title \emph{say} something. Same
lesson as the h-index and the relevance filter: the signal is in the contrast, not in the
absolute value.
\end{ojo}

\begin{practica}
The short title goes in the heading; the full centroid sentence is stored in the
\texttt{summary} column and shows up on \textbf{hover}: short on top, detail on mouseover,
exactly as you asked.
\end{practica}

\begin{resumen}
Cluster titles $=$ 2 to 3 word keyphrases extracted KeyBERT-style (candidates $+$ SPECTER $+$
cosine to the centroid $+$ MMR), with \texttt{min\_df} against the noise and a contrastive score
(cluster $-$ global) to prefer the specific. Short, meaningful, with no trimmed sentences; the
full sentence lives in the hover.
\end{resumen}
