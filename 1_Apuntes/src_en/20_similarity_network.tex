\section{How the network was built}

Each paper is connected to its \textbf{K most similar papers} (cosine similarity computed in
the \textbf{original 768D space}, not on the 2D map, which we already know distorts), and only
connections with a similarity $\geq$ a \textbf{threshold} survive. This is the so-called
\emph{kNN graph} (k nearest neighbours).

\begin{concepto}{the K and threshold knobs: nobody ``knows'' them, you test them}
You asked: who says 5 neighbours is ideal? Honest answer: \textbf{nobody}. It is a knob, like
granularity. The serious way to choose it is to try values and look at the resulting
structure:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rrrrr}
\textbf{K} & \textbf{lines} & \textbf{conn./paper} & \textbf{pieces} & \textbf{\% across clusters} \\
\hline
3 & 281 & 4.7 & 1 & 17\% \\
\textbf{5} & \textbf{457} & \textbf{7.6} & \textbf{1} & \textbf{21\%} \\
8 & 702 & 11.7 & 1 & 24\% \\
15 & 1213 & 20.2 & 1 & 30\% \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A high K reveals more crossings between topics (your first neighbours are family; the distant
ones cross borders) but with 20 connections per paper the drawing is unreadable (a
``hairball''). Chosen: \textbf{K=5, threshold 0.75}: readable, with visible bridges and the
network in a single piece. Both live in \codigo{config.py} (\codigo{GRAPH\_KNN},
\codigo{GRAPH\_SIM\_THRESHOLD}): changing your mind costs one line.
\end{concepto}

\section{Measuring hubs and bridges (no longer by eye)}

\begin{concepto}{degree and betweenness}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Hub} $=$ a node with many connections $\rightarrow$ measured by
        \textbf{degree} (count its edges).
  \item \textbf{Bridge} $=$ a node the paths between communities run through $\rightarrow$
        measured by \textbf{betweenness centrality}: how many times a node lies on the
        shortest path between two others. A paper with \emph{few} connections can be a great
        bridge if those few join worlds.
\end{itemize}
\end{concepto}
