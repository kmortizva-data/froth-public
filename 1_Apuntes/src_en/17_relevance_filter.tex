\cabecera{17}{The relevance filter: the system cleans itself}

\section{The problem YOU spotted}

While validating the map (step 3.7) you saw a cluster labelled \emph{``women, men, commodity,
masculinities''}. Diagnosis: the papers that infiltrated in Phase 1 (poetry,
telecommunications, history, from acronym collisions in the searches) dominated the keywords
of a cluster that also contained legitimate lithium economy papers. The root cause was in the
\emph{pull}, not in the map.

\section{The elegant fix: measure every paper against the TOPIC}

\begin{concepto}{relevance score}
We already have all the pieces: each paper has its vector, and the \textbf{title of your
thesis} can have one too. So: \emph{a paper's relevance} $=$ cosine similarity between its
vector and the TOPIC's vector. A poetry paper lands far from the title of a flotation thesis
$\rightarrow$ low score $\rightarrow$ out. The system uses \textbf{its own embeddings to clean
itself}: no manual rules, no blacklists.
\end{concepto}

\begin{concepto}{the threshold is dictated by the data, not by intuition}
Where to cut? We looked at the real distribution of the 126 scores:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
Infiltrators (poetry, telecom, history...) & 0.495 to 0.543 \\
\textbf{(natural gap)} & \\
Everything legitimate (including Li economy) & 0.587 to 0.90 \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
There is a \textbf{gap} between 0.543 and 0.587: the threshold
\codigo{RELEVANCE\_THRESHOLD = 0.55} falls in the middle and separates them perfectly. That is
how thresholds are chosen seriously: by looking at where the data is already separated, not by
inventing a nice-looking number.
\end{concepto}

\section{Where it lives in the code}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \codigo{embed.py} $\rightarrow$ \codigo{relevance\_to\_topic()}: computes the scores and
        saves them as a \codigo{relevance} column in \codigo{papers.parquet}.
  \item \codigo{config.py} $\rightarrow$ \codigo{RELEVANCE\_THRESHOLD = 0.55} (with the
        reasoning in a comment).
  \item \codigo{cluster.py} $\rightarrow$ filters BEFORE UMAP: the rubbish does not even take
        part in the arrangement, so the whole map improves.
\end{itemize}

\section{What changed on the map (a real data science iteration)}

\begin{itemize}
  \item The embarrassing labels disappeared; the economy cluster is now honest
        (\emph{usgs, old scrap} $=$ recycling/commodities).
  \item A \textbf{4th cluster} emerged that had been buried: literature \textbf{in French}
        about Nb-Ta-Sn recovery, probably the French school studying Beauvoir. A valuable
        finding, and a new lesson: \emph{language also groups} (SPECTER puts the French papers
        together). Future refinement: translate abstracts, or accept that cluster as a valid
        subtopic?
  \item The noise went up (9 $\rightarrow$ 21): as the map rearranged, more papers ended up
        between clouds. That is what your granularity slider is for.
\end{itemize}

\begin{ojo}
This is what the real data science cycle looks like: \textbf{build $\rightarrow$ look
$\rightarrow$ spot something odd $\rightarrow$ diagnose $\rightarrow$ fix $\rightarrow$
discover something new}. Your expert eye spotted the problem; the embeddings provided the
solution; and the fix uncovered a finding (the French cluster) that nobody was looking for.
Every iteration teaches.
\end{ojo}

\begin{resumen}
Relevance $=$ cosine(paper, TOPIC); threshold 0.55 chosen from the natural gap in the data;
filtered before UMAP. Result: a clean map, honest labels and a surprise French cluster about
Beauvoir. The system cleans itself with its own tools.
\end{resumen}
