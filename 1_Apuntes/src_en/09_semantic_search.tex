\cabecera{09}{The semantic search engine (putting it all together)}

\section{What we built}

A tool that takes a \textbf{phrase in natural language} and returns the papers closest
\emph{in meaning}, not by shared words. It is the \codigo{most\_similar()} function in
\codigo{froth/embed.py}, and it brings together everything learned in Phase 2.

\begin{concepto}{semantic search vs.\ keyword search}
Classic search (Ctrl+F, basic Google) matches \textbf{words}: if you search ``grain size'' it
does not find a text saying ``coarse and fine particles'', even though they mean the same
thing. \textbf{Semantic} search matches \textbf{meaning}: it turns your phrase into a vector
and looks for nearby vectors, so it does relate them.
\end{concepto}

\section{How it works inside (3 steps)}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Vectorise the phrase.} Your phrase goes through the \emph{same} model
        (SPECTER) with \codigo{model.encode([query])}. Key point: the phrase and the papers
        must live on the same map, or comparing them would make no sense.
  \item \textbf{Measure against everything.} With \codigo{cosine\_similarity} we compute the
        cosine of your phrase against all 126 vectors at once.
  \item \textbf{Keep the best.} We sort from highest to lowest cosine and return the first
        \codigo{k} (5 by default), as a \codigo{score, title, year} table.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{ojo}
The same model for both things. If you vectorised the papers with SPECTER and the phrase with
a different model, the two ``maps'' would not line up and the results would be rubbish.
Embedding consistency $=$ golden rule.
\end{ojo}

\section{The test (real results)}

Search: \emph{``how does grain size affect the recovery of minerals by froth flotation''}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\textbf{Score} & \textbf{Paper} \\
\hline
0.873 & Role of Bubble Size in Flotation of Coarse and Fine Particles \\
0.856 & Effect of Clay Slime on the Froth Stability and Flotation Performance \\
0.855 & Spodumene Flotation Mechanism \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
We typed ``grain size'' and it found papers about ``coarse and fine particles'' and
``ultrafine'': \textbf{it understood the meaning, not the words}.

Search: \emph{``extracting beryllium and tantalum as valuable by-products''}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\textbf{Score} & \textbf{Paper} \\
\hline
0.812 & Niobium and tantalum \\
0.740 & Concentration of Tantalum and Niobium \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
It brought back exactly the Ta/Nb/Be by-product papers from your thesis.

\begin{concepto}{a score between 0 and 1}
The \codigo{score} is the cosine similarity (Note 08). Here it hovers around 0.7 to 0.9:
high, and it makes sense. Careful: ``free phrase vs.\ paper'' scores tend to be somewhat
lower than ``paper vs.\ paper'' (0.94 earlier), because a short phrase is less rich than a
whole abstract. What matters is the \emph{ordering}, not the absolute number.
\end{concepto}

\section{What this means for the project}

You now have a genuinely useful tool: interrogating your corpus in natural language. And more
importantly: the machinery (vectorise $+$ cosine $+$ sort) is \textbf{the same} one that will
form the subtopic bubbles in Phase 3. Mastering this is mastering the core of Froth.

\begin{resumen}
The semantic search engine turns your phrase into a vector, compares it by cosine against the
126 papers and returns the closest in meaning. Tested: ``grain size'' found ``fine/coarse
particles''. Froth's engine is already beating.
\end{resumen}
