\section{What we did}

In Note 05 we saw the \emph{idea} of the embedding. Here we touch it for real: we take an
actual paper from your table and turn it into its vector.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item We load a paper (title $+$ abstract) from \codigo{2\_Datos/processed/papers.parquet}.
  \item We load the model in one line: \codigo{SentenceTransformer("allenai-specter")}.
  \item We convert it: \codigo{model.encode(text)} $\rightarrow$ a vector of \textbf{768 numbers}.
\end{enumerate}

The test paper was \emph{``Electrostatically Controlled Enrichment of Lepidolite via
Flotation''} and its vector started with \codigo{[-0.59, -0.16, 1.34, ...]}. That paper is
now a point on the map of meaning.

\begin{concepto}{pretrained model, in practice}
The first time you call \codigo{SentenceTransformer("allenai-specter")}, the library
\textbf{downloads} the model ($\sim$440 MB) from the Hugging Face repository and stores it in
a \emph{cache} on your disk. Later times it loads from there, instantly, with no internet.
You train nothing: you use weights other people already computed.
\end{concepto}

\begin{ojo}
\textbf{Title $+$ abstract, not the abstract alone.} SPECTER was trained to receive the title
glued to the abstract, because the title carries strong signal about the topic. That is why
the code does \codigo{text = title + ". " + abstract} before vectorising. A small detail that
improves the results.
\end{ojo}

\begin{ojo}
\textbf{The \emph{warnings} that came up are harmless.} One mentioned ``symlinks'' (a disk
optimisation on Windows) and the other ``HF\_TOKEN'' (an optional key for faster downloads).
Neither prevents anything: the model loaded and worked. Learn to tell a \emph{warning}
(a notice, carry on) from an \emph{error} (it stops).
\end{ojo}

\section{The key question: are we building the model?}

\textbf{No.} And it pays to be crystal clear about the difference between two things that
sound alike:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{p{4.3cm} p{4.3cm} p{4.3cm}}
 & \textbf{USING a model} & \textbf{TRAINING a model} \\[2pt]
\hline
What do you do? & You download a finished one and feed it text & You teach it from scratch with data; you adjust its ``weights'' \\[6pt]
What does it cost? & One line of code, seconds & Weeks, lots of data, graphics cards (GPUs) \\[6pt]
In Froth? & \textbf{Phases 2 to 5} (what we are doing now) & \textbf{Phase 6}, optional and advanced \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{concepto}{why reusing is the right call (for now)}
To draw the bubbles and the map, generic SPECTER, trained on millions of papers from every
field, is more than enough. Training your own embedding model from scratch would be
reinventing the wheel: hugely expensive and with no clear gain at this stage. Project rule:
\emph{recycle without shame}. What is original about your work is in \textbf{how you combine}
the pieces and the problem you solve, not in re-manufacturing the engine.
\end{concepto}

\section{So when do we actually touch ``the model''?}

In \textbf{Phase 6} (the last one, optional). There the experiment with thesis potential is
to \emph{fine-tune} a model with papers from your own domain (minerals, flotation,
metallurgy) and measure whether it produces \textbf{better} clusters than generic SPECTER.
That really is ``building something of your own'', and by then you will have learned PyTorch
and how a model works inside. It is dessert, not the main course.

\begin{resumen}
The vector model (SPECTER) is already built: we only download and use it, we do not train it.
Using $\neq$ training; training your own is Phase 6, optional. For now, relax: the engine is
already made.
\end{resumen}
